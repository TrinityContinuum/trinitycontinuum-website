What happens when the power grid fails and clean water is at risk?

Storms, damaged infrastructure and contamination events can interrupt essential utilities. Learn what to do immediately, what communities can plan for, and where coordinated water, power and thermal systems may fit into future infrastructure.

During an active emergency: safety comes first

Power outage

Follow local emergency-management and electric-utility instructions. Keep portable fuel-powered generators outdoors and away from windows; never connect a generator to building wiring without a properly installed transfer system.

Boil-water advisory

Follow your local water utility or health department. When officials instruct you to boil water, bring clear water to a rolling boil for one minute (three minutes above 6,500 feet). Use bottled water where advised.

Do-not-drink or do-not-use advisory

Do not assume boiling makes water safe: fuel, toxic chemicals and many other contaminants are not removed by boiling. Follow the exact advisory and use a safe alternative supply.

This page is not an emergency alert service. For an active emergency, follow your local utility, public-health and emergency-management authorities.

Questions communities and facility operators are asking

Can a facility keep operating when the grid goes down?

Local generation, batteries and properly engineered island-capable microgrids can be evaluated for critical loads. Transfer protection, fuel, permits and commissioning matter. A design proposal is not proof of emergency reliability.

Can a community produce clean water locally?

Water treatment and storage can form part of an emergency supply plan, but drinking water must meet applicable public-health standards and be independently tested. High-purity industrial water is not automatically approved as potable water.

What if electricity and water fail together?

Emergency planning should evaluate pumps, treatment, backup energy, distribution, storage, operator access and public-health coordination as one system—not as isolated equipment purchases.

Can industrial sites reduce stress on utilities?

Site-specific water reuse, on-site generation and thermal recovery may be worth studying. Savings, environmental effects and costs must be demonstrated with engineering and operating data.

Can data centers plan for these risks?

Yes. Facilities can assess cooling-water demand, power continuity, thermal loads, drought scenarios and community impacts before selecting an infrastructure configuration.

Who should participate?

Water utilities, electric utilities, emergency managers, public-health officials, engineers, residents and facility owners all have roles in responsible planning.
