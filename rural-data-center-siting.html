Does a data center have to be next to a city?

Not necessarily. Rural and industrial sites may offer alternatives when natural gas, a lawful water supply, suitable land and practical electrical interconnection can be secured. Trinity Continuum is developing modular water, power and thermal infrastructure for site-specific configurations.

Five feasibility gates before choosing a remote site

Natural gas

Confirm pipeline proximity, actual delivery capacity, pressure, tariffs, service rights and backup fuel strategy. A nearby pipeline alone is not a supply agreement.

Water

Verify permitted withdrawals, water quality, treatment requirements, storage, drought resilience and discharge or reuse pathways. Industrial high-purity water is not automatically potable.

Transmission

Check grid ownership, available capacity, substation requirements, interconnection studies, protection systems and costs. Existing wind-farm transmission corridors do not guarantee a new load connection.

Cooling and thermal loads

Model heat rejection, ambient climate, cooling-water demand and possible useful heat recovery under real operating conditions.

Land and community impacts

Evaluate zoning, access roads, noise, air permits, environmental review, neighbors, emergency response and local benefit agreements.

Commercial and reliability case

Compare capital and operating costs, fuel and water exposure, network connectivity, utility agreements and independently demonstrated reliability.

Regulatory and industry context

Large-load interconnection is under active review by the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission. In Texas, ERCOT's data-center impact review examines on-site generation, grid use, cooling and water consumption. These sources explain why infrastructure alternatives need detailed, independently reviewable feasibility work.
